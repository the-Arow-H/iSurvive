A bad site can wreck a good shelter. Check these before you commit.

## Overhead wood

The National Park Service camping page says to look up: if you see dead branches overhead, do not camp under them, and stay away from dead trees that might fall. Sequoia and Kings Canyon add that branches and trees may fall whether or not they are dead, and when there is no wind. Do not linger under dead, cracked, broken, or hanging branches, or under trees rotten at the base or shedding cracked bark. People call hanging dead limbs widowmakers. The park warning is the same idea without needing the nickname.

FM 3-05.70 says to look for loose rocks, dead limbs, and other growth that could fall on a natural shelter.

## Water and slope

The Park Service says to stay out of ravines that might flood, and FM 3-05.70 says to avoid flash-flood areas in the foothills and any site below the high-water mark. The FEMA appendix says to keep the shelter out of valleys, washes, troughs, and depressions, and to watch for avalanche slopes and rock that could collapse. FM 3-05.70 names avalanche and rockslide ground in the mountains as places to avoid.

Low ground is also colder at night. The Army manual says ravines, narrow valleys, and creek beds collect heavy cold air, and brushy low ground holds more insects.

## Storms, cliffs, and animals

The Park Service says not to stay on ledges or high peaks where wind and lightning are the hazard, and to pick a spot sheltered and away from the middle of a field in a lightning storm and away from the edge of cliffs. In bear country that page says to keep about 200 feet between cooking and sleeping, store food at least 200 feet from where you sleep, and follow the park's food-storage rule.

The FEMA appendix also says to avoid poison oak, ant nests, and animal dens, and to keep the shelter away from the cooking fire.

Make camp while you can still see. The Park Service says to learn the ground in daylight.
