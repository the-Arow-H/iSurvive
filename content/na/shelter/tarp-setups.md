A tarp, poncho, or similar sheet does not make heat. It sheds rain, cuts wind, or makes shade. Pair it with ground insulation from the priorities page.

## Lean-to

See the lean-to page for the Army poncho lean-to: back to the wind, drip sticks on the ridge lines, stakes on the low edge, a center support if it will sag.

## Low tent

FM 3-05.70 also describes a poncho tent between two trees about 2 to 3 meters apart, using two lengths of line and about six stakes. It covers both long sides and has a lower profile than a lean-to, with less room. Tie the hood the same way. A center prop or an A-frame over the middle keeps the roof off your chest. You still need a dry layer under you.

## Desert shade

In dry country the manual says to spend effort carefully. If you have a poncho, canvas, or similar cloth, use a rock outcrop, a sand mound, or the gap between dunes. Anchor one end on the rock or the mound with weight, and stretch the other end so the cloth actually shades you. For a beach or open sand trench, it says to pick a site above the high-water mark, dig a trench long enough to lie in, running north to south so it takes less sun, mound the spoil on three sides, and lay driftwood or poles across as a roof frame. Grass or leaves make the bed, not the bare sand.

## Ground cloth

The Park Service says a waterproof sheet under a tent works as a footprint if you fold the extra under. Cloth that sticks out past the tent floor collects rain and runs it underneath you.

Do not pitch any of these in a wash, under a widowmaker, or on a lightning-exposed peak. Those failures are on the site page, and a better knot will not fix them.
