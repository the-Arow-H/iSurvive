## Identification

The USDA NRCS plant guide (symbol TYLA) describes *Typha latifolia* as a herbaceous perennial in the cattail family, with rhizomes and long slender green stalks topped by brown, fluffy, sausage-shaped flowering heads. Plants are about 15 to 30 decimeters tall. The spike has staminate flowers above and pistillate flowers below. Basal leaves are thin with parallel veins running the long length of the leaf. Plants grow in colonies in wet ground.

## Season

The guide says young shoots are cut from the rhizomes in spring when they are about 4 to 16 inches long. Pollen is used when the male flowers shed it. Rhizomes are the starch storage. Do not pull a green shoot from a plant that has no sausage spike you can tie to that same clump.

## Range

The guide says broad-leaved cattails are common throughout the United States and in temperate and tropical places worldwide. Check the current USDA PLANTS map before you rely on a local stand. Wetland rules may prohibit digging.

## Preparation

The guide says all parts are edible when gathered at the right stage. Young shoots can be eaten raw or steamed. The base of the stem where it meets the rhizome can be boiled or roasted. Young flower stalks, taken out of their sheaths, can be boiled or steamed. Pollen can stand in for flour. Rhizomes and lower stems can be eaten raw, baked, roasted, or broiled, and the core can be ground into flour. The guide's starch figures (about 30% to 46%, and a flour yield per acre) are citations inside that guide, not something you should expect from one plant.

Wash anything that grew in dirty water, and cook it if the water around the stand was not treated. The water module still applies to the water you boil them in.

## DEADLY LOOK-ALIKES

The USDA guide used here does not name a deadly look-alike. That is not a clearance. Never eat this plant on a single identification. If there is no sausage-shaped flower head on the clump, and the leaves are not the long parallel-veined blades the guide describes, do not eat it. Flags, rushes, and bulrushes appear as alternate names in the guide. Those names are shared with other wetland plants. A shared nickname is a reason to stop, not a reason to eat.

## Rule

Never eat on a single identification. When in doubt, do not eat it.
