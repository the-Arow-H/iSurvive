## Identification

FEIS describes pawpaw as a native deciduous large shrub or small tree, about 20 to 40 feet (6 to 12 m), usually with a single trunk, growing in clones or thickets. Bark is thin with shallow irregular fissures. Young twigs are hairy. Leaves can be up to 1 foot (30 cm) long and smell when bruised. The fruit is a large berry. Flowers come out with the leaves.

## Season

Eat ripe fruit. FEIS says flowers emerge with the leaves from February in the southern part of the seasonal note, but it does not give one ripening month for the whole range. A hard green fruit is not the food described.

## Range

FEIS says pawpaw is widely distributed in the eastern United States: from western New York west across southwestern Ontario to Michigan, Illinois, and Iowa; south to eastern Nebraska, eastern Oklahoma, and eastern Texas; east to the Appalachians and the Florida panhandle. It grows in deciduous forests, on ravine slopes, and along streams.

## Preparation

FEIS says people eat the fruit raw, cooked in puddings or breads, or used in ice cream. Eat the pulp. Handling the fruit may cause allergic reactions in sensitive people. If your skin reacts, stop handling it and wash. Do not eat the seeds or the bark.

## DEADLY LOOK-ALIKES

FEIS says the seeds contain an alkaloid, asiminine, reported to have emetic properties, and that the bark contains an alkaloid, analobine. The hazard named here is the plant's own seeds and bark, not a second species. Never eat this plant on a single identification. If the bruised leaf does not smell and the fruit is not the large berry on this clonal understory tree, do not eat it. This guide does not name another species as a pawpaw double because the review does not.

## Rule

Never eat on a single identification. When in doubt, do not eat it.
