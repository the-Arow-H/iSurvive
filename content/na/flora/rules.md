Never eat a wild plant on a single identification. Use several features, from the plant in front of you, and reject it if one feature is wrong. A photo in this guide is not included in v0.1. Words are not enough when two species share a leaf shape. If you are not sure, do not eat it.

This guide does not cover mushrooms. Do not use it to identify a fungus.

## Poison hemlock

USDA Agricultural Research Service says poison hemlock (*Conium maculatum*) grows through the United States and is extremely poisonous to people. All parts are poisonous: leaves, stem, fruit, and root. People are often poisoned after mistaking the root for wild parsnip, the leaves for parsley, or the seed for anise. Whistles made from the hollow stems have killed children. Do not eat any member of the carrot family from this guide. This guide has no wild-carrot card on purpose.

## Water hemlock

ARS calls water hemlock (*Cicuta*, including *Cicuta douglasii*) the most violently toxic plant that grows in North America. A small amount can poison a person. The underground parts, especially the tuberous roots, are highly toxic. It has been confused with wild parsnips and other herbs. If you think someone has eaten it, contact a poison center and get emergency medical help. Do not wait for a field remedy. This guide gives none.

## Death camas

ARS says death camas (*Zigadenus* species, also discussed now in *Toxicoscordion*) has grasslike leaves from a bulb and yellowish-white flowers. The bulbs are reported to cause severe illness and death in people. If a bulb was eaten, ARS says the effects may be lethal and the person needs emergency treatment. This guide does not teach wild onions. A bulb in the ground is not a free meal.

## Yew

ARS describes yew (*Taxus*) as an evergreen shrub or small tree with narrow, stiff, pointed leaves about 1.25 to 2.5 cm long and a scarlet (sometimes yellow) fleshy fruit. Humans are susceptible. Taxine is the toxic alkaloid ARS names. Do not make tea from yew needles, and do not eat the fruit. This guide does not teach pine-needle tea. The pine pages in the Forest Service reviews used here describe wood or, for pinyon, seeds, not a needle tea, so a needle tea is not a technique this edition will invent. If you cannot tell a yew from another conifer, you are not making a drink out of either.

The plant cards that follow are foods only where a USDA or USFS page says so. Each card has its own deadly-look-alike block. Read that block before the preparation section.
