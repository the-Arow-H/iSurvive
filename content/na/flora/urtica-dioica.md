## Identification

FEIS describes stinging nettle as an erect perennial that forms dense clones. Stems are about 3.3 to 6.6 feet (1 to 2 m). Leaves, stems, and flowers are sparsely to moderately covered with stinging hairs. The fruit is an achene. American and hoary subspecies are native. European stinging nettle is introduced.

## Season

FEIS does not give a month. Use leaves from the growing plant, and cook them. Gloves or a folded cloth keep the hairs off your skin while you pick.

## Range

FEIS says American stinging nettle is the common subspecies through temperate North America: much of Canada and the United States, south in the East and Midwest to Virginia, Missouri, and Kansas, and in the West south along the coast to central California and in the Rockies to Mexico. European stinging nettle is mainly along the Atlantic coast from Newfoundland to Georgia and Alabama. It is listed as a noxious weed in several Canadian provinces. Harvest rules may apply.

## Preparation

FEIS says boiled stinging nettle leaves are edible and can be substituted for spinach. Boil them. Do not eat them raw. The hairs are irritating to human skin, and the pollen is a hay-fever source. Medicinal uses are mentioned and are not taught here.

## DEADLY LOOK-ALIKES

The FEIS review used here does not name a second species as a deadly look-alike. The hazard it does name is the plant itself. Stinging hairs hurt skin. Never eat this plant on a single identification, and never eat it uncooked. If the hairs and the upright clonal growth are not both there, it is not the plant in the review. A plant that only looks vaguely leafy is not nettle.

## Rule

Never eat on a single identification. When in doubt, do not eat it.
