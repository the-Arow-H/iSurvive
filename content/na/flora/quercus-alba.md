## Identification

FEIS describes white oak as a medium to large deciduous tree, commonly 60 to 80 feet (18 to 24 m), taller on good sites. It is monoecious. Yellowish staminate catkins are at the base of new growth. Pistillate flowers are in the axils of new growth. Acorns are short-stalked, glabrous, ovoid, and tan to brown.

## Season

Use mature acorns. FEIS does not give a single drop date for the whole range. Collect when the nuts are full and tan to brown, not when they are green mush.

## Range

FEIS says white oak grows through much of the eastern United States, from southwest Maine to northern Florida, Alabama, and Georgia, west through southern Ontario and Quebec into the Great Lakes states and south to eastern Kansas, eastern Oklahoma, and eastern Texas. Confirm the tree on the USDA PLANTS map. Acorns from other oaks are not this card.

## Preparation

FEIS says acorns were a traditional food for many Native American peoples. They have been described as sweet and edible and also as slightly bitter. They were often boiled to remove bitter tannins. Boil pieces in changes of water until the bitterness is gone, then cook them as a food. The review does not give a minute count. Taste is the check it implies by "bitter," and you still throw out nuts that are moldy or insect-hollow. This card does not teach acorn oil as a pain treatment. That medicinal line in FEIS is not a field dose.

## DEADLY LOOK-ALIKES

USDA ARS lists oak (*Quercus*) among poisonous plants. Livestock are poisoned by oak browse. Do not eat leaves, buds, or bark. Do not eat raw acorns and call them finished. Never eat an acorn on a single identification of the tree. If you cannot show that the tree is a white oak, do not use this card for its nuts. This guide does not name a second species as a look-alike beyond that limit, because the FEIS review does not.

## Rule

Never eat on a single identification. When in doubt, do not eat it.
