## Identification

The USDA NRCS plant guide titled blue elderberry gives the name *Sambucus nigra* L. ssp. *canadensis* and the symbol SANIC4. It says recent taxonomy has moved and that the plant was previously split into several western names, including *Sambucus caerulea*, and is called *Sambucus mexicana* in some floras. Leaves are pinnately compound, with 2 to 4 paired leaflets plus a terminal leaflet, opposite each other on the branch. Flowers are white or cream. The berries this guide allows are blue or purple. Seedlings and shrubs without flowers or fruit can resemble California walnut and Oregon ash. Do not identify it from leaves alone.

## Season

That guide says clusters are gathered in July and August in the California practice it describes, then dried or cooked. Do not pick red fruit and hope the month makes it safe.

## Range

The guide is written around western riparian stands and the California floristic setting, including valley oak riparian habitat of the valley elderberry longhorn beetle. Do not treat this card as a license to eat every elderberry in the East. Check the USDA PLANTS map for the symbol, and still require blue or purple fruit on a plant you have identified with more than one feature. The beetle is federally listed. Do not cut occupied shrubs to get a snack.

## Preparation

The guide says only the blue or purple berries are edible. They are made into wine, jam, syrup, and pies, and the guide also records drying and later cooking, and some dried fruit eaten without further cooking. It also says the active alkaloids hydrocyanic acid and sambucine will cause nausea, so care is required. Cook the berries. Do not eat leaves, bark, or red fruit. Folk-medicine uses of flowers, leaves, and juice are printed in that guide and are not taught here. There is no dose in this guide.

## DEADLY LOOK-ALIKES

The USDA guide says the red berries of other elderberry species are toxic and should not be gathered. That is the deadly look-alike block. Never eat this plant on a single identification. If the fruit is red, or you are not sure it is blue or purple and ripe, do not eat it. If the shrub has no fruit and no flowers, the leaves are not enough, because the guide says they can resemble walnut and ash. Water hemlock is a separate deadly plant and does not look like a berry shrub. It is still a reason to stay out of unidentified wetland roots. Read the flora rules page.

## Rule

Never eat on a single identification. When in doubt, do not eat it.
