## Identification

FEIS describes black cherry as a deciduous tree. In eastern forest it often has a straight, branch-free trunk and a narrow crown. In the open the trunk is shorter and the crown broader. Typical eastern trees (var. *serotina*) may reach 125 feet (38 m). Southwestern varieties are smaller. Mature bark is shown in the FEIS review as dark and plated. Confirm bark, leaves, and fruit together. This card does not reduce the tree to "a tree with cherries."

## Season

Use ripe fruit. FEIS says pitted fruits are edible and are eaten raw and used in wine and jelly. It does not give one ripening date for every variety.

## Range

FEIS says black cherry grows in eastern North America from western Minnesota south to eastern Texas and east to the Atlantic from central Florida to Nova Scotia, with outlying populations in central Texas, the mountains of western Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona, and south in Mexico to Guatemala.

## Preparation

Remove the pit. Eat or cook the flesh. Do not crush seeds into the jelly. FEIS notes the bark was used historically as a cough remedy. This guide does not teach bark medicine. There is no dose, and bark is not food.

## DEADLY LOOK-ALIKES

USDA ARS lists chokecherry (*Prunus* spp.) among cyanide-containing poisonous plants. Black cherry is a *Prunus*. Do not eat leaves, bark, or seeds. Wilted foliage is a known livestock hazard for this genus in that ARS grouping. Never eat this plant on a single identification. A cherry you cannot separate from another *Prunus* still follows the same limit: flesh of ripe fruit only, pits out, and only if you are sure of the tree. If you are not sure, do not eat it.

## Rule

Never eat on a single identification. When in doubt, do not eat it.
