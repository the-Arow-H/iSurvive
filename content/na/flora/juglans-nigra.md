## Identification

FEIS describes black walnut as a native deciduous tree, often around 80 feet (25 m) and capable of 125 feet (38 m). In the forest the trunk is long and the crown small. In the open the trunk forks low. Young bark is dark and scaly. Older bark is darker with rounded intersecting ridges. The nut is the food part the review names. Match the tree, not a lone nut on the ground that could have rolled from somewhere else.

## Season

Harvest mature nuts. FEIS says flowering begins about mid-April in the southern part of its seasonal note. It does not give one drop date. Use nuts that are full, not moldy and not insect dust.

## Range

FEIS says black walnut grows through the eastern United States, north to southern Minnesota, southern Wisconsin, southern Michigan, and southern Ontario, with isolated populations in southern New York, Vermont, western Massachusetts, and northwestern Connecticut, south to northwestern Florida and through Mississippi, Arkansas, and Louisiana except the Mississippi Valley and Delta, and with isolated Midwest populations in eastern Texas, western Oklahoma, and central Kansas.

## Preparation

FEIS says people eat the nuts plain or with honey and use them to flavor cakes, candy, and ice cream, and that Native Americans used the nuts for food. Husk them, dry them, and crack the shell. The green hull stains skin and is not described as the food. A dye from the roots is also not food.

## DEADLY LOOK-ALIKES

The FEIS review used here does not name a deadly look-alike. That is not a clearance. Never eat this nut on a single identification. If you cannot tie the nut to a black walnut tree with the bark and form above, do not eat it. Do not eat nuts that smell of mold.

## Rule

Never eat on a single identification. When in doubt, do not eat it.
