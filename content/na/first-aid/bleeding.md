**NOT MEDICAL ADVICE.** Take a Stop the Bleed class if you can: [stopthebleed.org](https://www.stopthebleed.org). The steps below are the public Ready.gov sequence and a short paraphrase of the public Red Cross pages, not those courses.

Ready.gov's one-page card says:

1. Find the source of the bleeding. There may be more than one wound.
2. If you have something to put between the blood and your hands, use it. Gloves, a cloth, or a plastic bag are the examples on the card.
3. Apply firm, steady pressure directly on the source. Push hard, even if it hurts the person.
4. Keep that pressure until emergency medical services arrive.

The longer Ready.gov tutorial says a bandage alone soaks up blood. Pressure is what stops bleeding. Hold it until crews take over, and trade off with another person if your hands tire. Call for help. Move someone only to get them out of further danger.

The public Red Cross bleeding page says the urgent move is pressure right away, with hands or with cloth, and that life-threatening bleeding on an arm or leg can take a tourniquet if you have one and know how. It also says wound packing is for people who are trained, for life-threatening bleeding of the scalp, neck, shoulder, groin, or back, or of a limb when no tourniquet is available. This guide does not teach packing. Get the class.

Ready.gov describes a tourniquet as another way to put firm pressure on an arm or leg when the bleeding will not stop otherwise, and its quiz answer for a wound near the knee is to place the tourniquet above the wound. The public Red Cross tourniquet article says to place a commercial tourniquet 2 to 3 inches above the wound, between the wound and the heart, not on the wound and not on a joint, to follow the device instructions, and not to loosen or remove it. A medical professional does that. If the first tourniquet is as tight as it will go and the bleeding has not stopped, that article says a second one can go above the first, closer to the heart.

Do not wait to see if bleeding slows on its own when it is spurting or soaking through pressure. That is a reason to evacuate, and it is on the evacuate page.

This page does not give a time limit for how long a tourniquet may stay on, and it does not give a drug dose.
