**NOT MEDICAL ADVICE.** Ready.gov is the source for this page.

Ready.gov describes three depths in plain language. First-degree burns affect the top layer of skin and may be red, swollen, and painful. Second-degree burns reach the thicker lower layer and may blister, swell, and hurt. Third-degree burns go through the skin into tissue underneath. Ready.gov says third-degree burns need immediate medical attention. The burned skin may be dry and leathery, with white, brown, or black patches.

For a minor burn, Ready.gov says:

- Remove clothing, jewelry, and metal from the burned area. They hold heat. Do not peel off anything that is stuck.
- Use cool water, not cold water and not ice. Ice can add injury.
- Especially for a chemical burn, hold the skin under cool running water for 10 to 15 minutes, until it hurts less.
- If you have no running water, use a cool clean wet cloth, or a cool bath for five minutes.
- Clean gently with soap and water.
- Do not break blisters. An open blister can get infected.
- A thin layer of petroleum jelly or aloe vera is allowed on that page. The ointment does not need an antibiotic in it. Do not use cream, lotion, oil, cortisone, butter, or egg white.
- Cover with sterile non-stick gauze that will not shed fibers. Change it once a day.

This guide does not name an antibiotic ointment and does not give a pain-medicine dose. Ready.gov's "when to get help" list is the evacuate rule for burns:

- Dark red, glossy, and heavily blistered.
- Larger than about two inches.
- From fire, electricity, or chemicals.
- Dry and leathery, or with white, brown, or black patches.
- Signs of infection: more pain, redness, swelling, discharge, fever, swollen lymph nodes, or a red streak.

Ready.gov also says burns are susceptible to tetanus, and that a person whose last tetanus shot was more than five years ago may need a booster. That decision is a clinician's. Do not guess a vaccine dose in the field.
