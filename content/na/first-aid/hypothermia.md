**NOT MEDICAL ADVICE.** Hypothermia is a medical emergency on the CDC winter page.

CDC says hypothermia can happen in very cold air, and also above 40°F if the person is wet from rain, sweat, or cold water. A low body temperature affects thinking, so the person may not know it is happening.

Adult warning signs on that page: shivering, exhaustion, confusion, fumbling hands, memory loss, slurred speech, drowsiness. In babies: bright red, cold skin and very low energy. NIOSH lists late signs that include no shivering, blue skin, dilated pupils, slow pulse and breathing, and loss of consciousness. Shivering that stops is not a sign that the person is fine.

If you can take a temperature and it is below 95°F, CDC says to get medical attention immediately.

If you cannot get help right away, CDC says:

- Get the person into a warm room or shelter.
- Remove wet clothing.
- Warm the center of the body — chest, neck, head, and groin — with an electric blanket if you have one, or with skin-to-skin contact under loose, dry layers.
- Warm drinks can help if the person is conscious. Do not give alcohol. Do not try to give a drink to someone who is unconscious.
- Once temperature has come up, keep them dry and wrapped, including head and neck, and still get medical care.

For severe hypothermia, CDC says the person may be unconscious and may not seem to have a pulse or to be breathing. Handle them gently, get emergency help, and give CPR even if they look dead, continuing until they respond or medical aid takes over. Keep warming them during CPR. This guide does not teach CPR. Use training you already have, and the emergency number.

Frostbite, from the same CDC page: do not walk on frostbitten feet unless you have to. Do not warm frostbite on a stove, radiator, heat lamp, fireplace, heating pad, or electric blanket. Do not rub it. CDC says to put the frostbitten area in warm-to-touch water, not hot water you cannot judge. Numb skin burns easily.

Shelter and dry layers are the prevention side. They are in the shelter module. This page is the care side, and it is still awaiting clinical review.
