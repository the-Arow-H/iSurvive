**NOT MEDICAL ADVICE.**

## Venomous snake

NIOSH says to get emergency care as soon as possible so antivenom can be started if it is needed. Do not drive yourself if the bite can make you dizzy or pass out. A photo from a safe distance may help. Do not pick up the snake, including a dead one or a severed head.

While you wait:

- Lay or sit with the bite in a neutral position of comfort.
- Take off rings and watches before swelling starts.
- Wash with soap and water.
- Cover with a clean, dry dressing.
- Mark the edge of tenderness or swelling and write the time next to it.

NIOSH says not to: wait for symptoms, apply a tourniquet, use electric shock or folk remedies, cut the wound, suck the venom, apply ice or soak the wound, drink alcohol as a painkiller, or take aspirin, ibuprofen, or naproxen. This page names those drugs only as items NIOSH says not to use. It does not give a dose of anything else.

## Tick

CDC says to remove an attached tick as soon as you can. Do not wait for a clinic to do it.

1. Grasp the tick as close to the skin as you can with clean fine-tipped tweezers. Regular tweezers or fingers are the backup. Do not squeeze the body.
2. Pull upward with steady, even pressure. Do not twist or jerk. If the mouthparts break off and you cannot lift them easily, leave them. The skin pushes them out as it heals.
3. Kill the loose tick in alcohol, tape, a sealed container, or the toilet. Do not crush it in your fingers.
4. Clean the bite and your hands with soap and water, rubbing alcohol, or sanitizer.
5. Check the rest of the body. More than one tick is common.

Do not use petroleum jelly, heat, nail polish, or other substances to make the tick let go. CDC says that can agitate the tick.

If a rash or fever shows up in the days to weeks after a bite, see a clinician and say when and where the tick bite happened. This guide does not give an antibiotic dose. Prophylaxis decisions are on CDC clinician pages and are not repeated here.

## Mammals

CDC's rabies page says people in the United States are usually exposed by wild animals, commonly raccoons, skunks, bats, and foxes. Do not approach an animal that looks injured, sick, or dead, especially one that is out in daylight when that species is normally nocturnal. A bite or scratch needs medical care urgently. Do not try to catch the animal. Rabies care is time-sensitive and is not a field recipe in this guide.
