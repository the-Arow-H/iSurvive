These are small sources. They do not replace a treated creek when you have one.

## Dew

The FEMA US&R appendix says rain can be caught on plastic and in any cup you set out, and that a blanket can be spread and wrung. It says dew can be collected the same way. Wring the cloth into a container. Then treat that water. Dew on a dirty cloth is not sterile, and CDC's rule is that you treat water unless you already know it is safe.

## Sheet still

The same FEMA appendix describes a still: an open damp spot, a hole about 3 feet deep, a cup in the center, a plastic sheet about 6 by 6 feet sealed at the edges with the spoil, and a rock on the sheet so it sags over the cup. Moisture condenses on the underside and drips in. The appendix says vegetation or urine can be added to the hole. It also says the water collected this way is safe without further purification.

Read that next to CDC, which is the newer public-health page. CDC says boiling is the surest way to kill germs, and that you should not drink water that may hold fuel or chemicals. A dirty sheet, a cup that held something else, or fuel in the soil can contaminate condensate. If you can boil, boil what the still collects. Do not drink urine, and do not count on this hole for a group's daily water. The FEMA appendix's yield figure is not repeated here.

A bag tied over a live leafy branch is a transpiration bag. That method is not described in the FEMA appendix or the CDC pages used here, so this guide does not give bag sizes or wait times for it. Use the sheet still those pages do describe, or treat surface water.
