CDC says chemical disinfectants kill most germs but do not work as well as boiling against some parasites, including Cryptosporidium and Giardia. Chlorine bleach and iodine kill bacteria and viruses and do not work well on parasites. Chlorine dioxide tablets can kill Giardia and are somewhat effective on Cryptosporidium if you follow the label. Filter first when you can.

If the water is cloudy, murky, colored, or very cold, CDC says to double the bleach dose below. Strain or settle cloudy water first.

Use unscented household bleach. Do not use scented, color-safe, or bleach with added cleaners. Read the label. If it has drinking-water directions, follow those. If it does not, use the sodium hypochlorite strength on the label.

## Bleach at 5% to 9%

CDC's emergency page, after stirring, wait at least 30 minutes:

- 1 quart or liter: 2 drops (about 0.1 mL).
- 1 gallon: 8 drops (about 1/2 mL, a little less than 1/8 teaspoon).
- 5 gallons: 40 drops (about 2 1/2 mL, or 1/2 teaspoon).

## Bleach at 1%

Same wait. This is more drops because the bleach is weaker:

- 1 quart or liter: 10 drops (about 1/2 mL, or 1/8 teaspoon).
- 1 gallon: 40 drops (about 2 1/2 mL, or 1/2 teaspoon).
- 5 gallons: 200 drops (about 12 1/2 mL, or 2 1/2 teaspoons).

EPA's emergency page adds a check some kits use: after 30 minutes the water should smell slightly of chlorine. If it does not, repeat the dose and wait another 15 minutes. If the chlorine taste is strong, pour the water between clean containers and let it stand.

## Iodine

CDC says to follow the label on iodine tablets or drops. EPA describes one common backup: 5 drops of 2% tincture of iodine per quart or liter, or 10 drops if the water is cloudy or colored, then stand at least 30 minutes.

CDC says people who are pregnant, who have thyroid problems, or who are hypersensitive to iodine should not drink iodine-treated water. No one should drink it for more than a few weeks at a time.

Iodine and iodine tablets do not kill Cryptosporidium. Neither does chlorine alone. Boiling does.

These drop counts are disinfection instructions printed by CDC and EPA for emergency drinking water. They are not medicine doses for a person.
