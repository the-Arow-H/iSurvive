Small game means animals a state defines as small game: often rabbit, squirrel, and similar species. What is legal, and in which month, is not national. The Fish and Wildlife Service says you generally need the license of the state you are in and must follow that state's seasons and methods. Read them before you hunt. This page is about disease, not about how to evade a warden.

CDC says tularemia spreads through tick and deer-fly bites, contact with infected animals, contaminated water, and dust. Hunters should use gloves when handling animals, especially rabbits, muskrats, prairie dogs, and other rodents, and should cook game meat thoroughly. Do not handle animals that are sick or already dead if you do not have to. The Missouri Department of Conservation says to wear gloves while cleaning rabbits, to look at the liver, and to discard a rabbit with many pinhead-sized white spots on the liver. It says to cook rabbit to at least 165°F.

FoodSafety.gov lists rabbit and venison, wild or farm-raised, at 160°F (71°C). CDC's 2022 trichinellosis investigation says cooking wild game to at least 165°F (74°C) is what kills *Trichinella*, and that color is not a doneness test. When those numbers differ, use 165°F for wild mammals and use a thermometer. The cooking page explains the two citations side by side.

Rabies: CDC says not to approach wildlife that looks injured, sick, or dead. Raccoons, skunks, bats, and foxes are the usual reservoirs named on that page. Do not eat an animal that was acting sick. A bite or scratch is a medical emergency, not a meal.

Birds that are legal game are poultry once they are in the pot. FSIS says all poultry goes to 165°F. That is on the cooking page too.
