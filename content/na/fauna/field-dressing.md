This is food-handling, not a hunting lesson. New York State's game page and CDC's tularemia page agree on the protective habits.

- Wear gloves. Nitrile, rubber, or latex are the examples New York gives. CDC stresses gloves for rabbits, muskrats, prairie dogs, and other rodents. Bare hands with cuts are a bad combination with blood.
- Do not eat, drink, or smoke while you are cleaning the animal.
- Get the intestines out and do not puncture them. Keep intestinal contents off the meat. Discard the guts away from water you will treat and away from camp.
- Look at a rabbit liver. Missouri says numerous pinhead white spots are a reason to throw the rabbit out and not eat it.
- Remove bullets, slugs, shot, and the bruised meat around them.
- Do not cut through the skull or the spinal cord. New York says to avoid brain, spinal tissue, saliva, and mouth parts. That is a rabies and chronic-wasting precaution. CDC says not to handle an animal that was acting sick. If it was, do not turn it into food. Get medical care if you were bitten or scratched.
- Wash hands, knife, and the work surface with soap and water when you finish. New York also describes a bleach rinse for tools. Keep a knife you use on carcasses separate from the knife you use on food that is ready to eat.
- Keep the carcass cool. New York says ice or refrigeration below 45°F (7°C) until it is butchered, then refrigerate or freeze until you cook it. In hot weather that means shade and speed, not a day in a pack.

Wash water is still untreated surface water until you treat it. Do not rinse a carcass in the creek you drink from and then skip the water pages.

Cooking temperatures are on the next page. Field dressing does not make rare meat safe.
